# Teaching and explaining

Read this when the reader must come away understanding something they did not understand before: a concept explained in a reply, a walkthrough, a tutorial, onboarding, the "how it works" part of a page or pitch.

Each instruction gives when it applies, what to do, and how to check it.

## Find where the reader stands

### Name what the reader already knows

- When: before any explanation.
- Do: write down, for yourself, what this reader can already do and which words they already use. Take it from what they said, the question's wording, and the artifact they work in. Where you cannot tell, ask one question, or write for a capable newcomer and say that you did.
- Check: you can name the first term in your draft that this reader has not met, and the draft defines it there.

### Correct for your own knowledge

- When: you know the subject well. Knowing it makes its steps feel obvious and its jargon feel like plain language.
- Do: list every step you would do without thinking and every term you learned on the job, then put each on the page. Spell out the step between two steps that "follows".
- Check: a reader who knows only what you named in the instruction above could carry out or restate each step without asking what a word means.

### Find the misconception first

- When: the subject has a common wrong model, or the reader's question shows one.
- Do: state the wrong model in one sentence, say which part of it fails and where the confusion comes from, then give the right model. Keep the wrong model to that one sentence.
- Check: the explanation names the specific belief it replaces.

## Order the explanation

### Say where you are going

- When: the explanation runs past a few sentences.
- Do: open with one or two sentences that give the idea at its most general and say what the reader will be able to do at the end. Tie it to something the reader already knows.
- Check: a reader who stops after the opening can say what the thing is for.

### Teach the parts before the whole machine

- When: a process or system has named parts the reader has not met.
- Do: introduce each part with its name and its one job, then show how the parts work together. Introduce at most four new parts before using them.
- Check: no sentence in the "how it works" section uses a part's name before the part was introduced.

### Cut into segments the reader controls

- When: the explanation has more than about four steps or ideas.
- Do: break it into segments of one idea each, each with a label. In an interactive page, let the reader move to the next segment themselves. In a reply, use short paragraphs or numbered steps, one idea each.
- Check: each segment can be summarised in one sentence, and no segment needs a later one to make sense.

### Go from concrete to abstract

- When: the idea is abstract: a rule, a formula, a pattern, a type, a policy.
- Do: start with one concrete case with real names and numbers. Then show the same case with the incidental detail stripped away. Then state the general rule. Keep all three visibly linked: same order, same labels.
- Check: the abstract statement appears only after a case, and every symbol or term in it maps to something the reader saw in the case.

## Build the idea

### Work an example in full

- When: the reader is new to a procedure or a kind of problem.
- Do: show one example solved step by step, with the reason for each step. Follow it with a second example where the last step is left to the reader, then one where more is left.
- Check: the first example leaves no step for the reader to work out, and the steps name their reasons.

### Give more than one example, and one that is not

- When: the reader must recognise a concept in the wild.
- Do: give two or three examples that differ in surface detail and share the structure. Add one near miss that looks like the concept and is not, and say what it lacks.
- Check: the examples differ in something irrelevant, and the near miss differs in something that matters.

### Use an analogy for structure, and mark where it breaks

- When: the new idea has the same relations as something the reader knows.
- Do: map the parts explicitly: this corresponds to that. State one place where the analogy fails. Drop the analogy once the real terms are in use.
- Check: the mapping is written out, and its limit is stated.

### Show it in a second form

- When: the idea has a spatial, quantitative or temporal shape that words alone make the reader build in their head.
- Do: add a second representation that carries that shape: a picture, a table, a small chart, a worked number, a control to move. Place it beside the words it belongs to and use the same terms in both. `multiple-channels.md` holds the rules.
- Check: the second form shows something the words do not, and each of its labels appears in the text.

### Let the reader predict before you reveal

- When: the medium allows a pause: a conversation, an interactive page, a document with collapsible parts.
- Do: ask what the reader expects to happen before showing what does. In a reply, pose the question and give the answer after a clear break.
- Check: the prediction can be wrong in an informative way; the answer explains why.

## Make it stick

### Ask the reader to retrieve it

- When: the reader needs to keep the idea, as in training material, onboarding and tutorials.
- Do: end a segment with a question that the reader can answer only by using the idea. Put the answer where the reader must act to see it. Prefer a question that applies the idea to a new case over one that asks for a definition.
- Check: the question cannot be answered by copying a sentence from the page.

### Ask for an explanation in the reader's own words

- When: you are in dialogue with a learner.
- Do: after a step, ask why it works or what would happen if one thing changed. Respond to what they say before going on.
- Check: your next message builds on their answer.

### Summarise what changed in the reader's model

- When: at the end of an explanation longer than a screen.
- Do: close with the rule or model in one or two sentences, in the terms the explanation built. Add the one next thing to try.
- Check: the summary uses no term the explanation did not define.

## Fit the reader's level

### Give experts less scaffolding

- When: the reader already knows the field. Step-by-step guidance and repeated definitions slow an expert down and can make their understanding worse.
- Do: for an expert, lead with the result or the difference from what they know, give the rule before the example, and move worked steps behind a link or a collapsed section. For a newcomer, keep the worked steps in line.
- Check: the level you chose is stated by something on the page: the opening names who it is for, or the detail is one click away for the other group.

### Serve a mixed audience in layers

- When: newcomers and experts will read the same artifact.
- Do: put the point where everyone gets it, in plain words. Under it, put the layer most readers need. Put background for newcomers and depth for experts in labelled sections each can skip or open.
- Check: an expert can reach the new information without reading a definition they know, and a newcomer can reach every definition without leaving the page.

### Offer the same idea more than one way, to everyone

- When: readers differ in how they take things in, which is always.
- Do: offer the same content in more than one form and let each reader pick: words and a picture, an example and a rule, a table and a chart, something to read and something to try. Give every reader every form; do not sort readers into types and hand each type one form.
- Check: each form can stand alone for a reader who uses only that one.

## Check list

Before sending an explanation, confirm each of these on the draft:

1. The opening says what the thing is for, in words the reader already has.
2. Every new term is defined where it first appears.
3. A concrete case comes before the general rule.
4. No more than about four new things are introduced before they are used.
5. A second representation carries any shape that words make the reader imagine.
6. The reader has something to do with the idea: a prediction, a question, a next step.
7. The level matches the reader, and the other level's material is reachable.
