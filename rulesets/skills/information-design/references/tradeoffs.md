# Where good advice conflicts

Read this when two instructions in this skill, or two pieces of well-known design advice, pull in opposite directions. Each entry names the two sides and the condition under which each holds. Find the row that matches the case and follow that side.

## Plain or embellished

| Follow | When |
| --- | --- |
| Remove everything that is not data or a label | the reader is analysing, monitoring, or looking something up: documentation, dashboards, reports, working charts |
| Add one relevant image, pictogram or strong visual treatment | the artifact has one message for a broad audience and must be noticed and remembered: a pitch, a poster, a public explainer |

In both cases the values must read the same with the embellishment removed, and nothing may cover or distort the data.

## Does the value axis start at zero

| Follow | When |
| --- | --- |
| Start at zero | the mark's length or area is the value: bars, columns, areas. Also when the reader should judge a change against the whole amount |
| Fit the range to the data | position carries the value: lines, dots. Also when the meaningful variation is small relative to the total, as with body temperature or an index |

When the range is fitted, label the ticks clearly and state the numbers of the change in words.

## Pie chart or bars

| Follow | When |
| --- | --- |
| A pie, a donut or a single stacked bar | one whole, two or three parts, and the reader needs rough fractions: about half, about a quarter |
| Sorted bars | four or more parts, or the reader must compare parts with each other, or there are several wholes |

Label slices directly in either case.

## Message title or neutral title

| Follow | When |
| --- | --- |
| The title states the finding | you are presenting a conclusion the data shows as displayed; the reader will retain the title above everything else |
| The title neutrally describes the data | the reader is exploring or checking; the chart is reference material; the topic is contested and a stated conclusion would steer the reading |

Under a message title, the subtitle still carries the neutral description: measure, unit, scope, period.

## Direct labels or a legend

| Follow | When |
| --- | --- |
| Label the marks directly | up to about four series, and there is room beside the marks |
| Use a legend, ordered as the marks are ordered | labels would collide or cover data; or the same key serves many small panels |

With many series, the better move is often to highlight a few and label only those.

## Error bars or another form

| Follow | When |
| --- | --- |
| An interval mark with its kind and level stated | the audience is trained in statistics and expects it |
| Countable outcomes, a labelled band, or a spread of sample outcomes | a general audience; or any audience when the decision depends on how likely a value is |

Never draw an interval on a bar, where the bar's body reads as "likely" and the space above it as "unlikely".

## Interactive or static

| Follow | When |
| --- | --- |
| Static, with the message complete on the page | the reader needs one conclusion; the artifact may be printed, pasted or skimmed; you cannot test the interaction |
| Interactive on top of a complete static message | the reader's own assumptions or own slice of the data change the answer; the data is too large for one view; the reader is learning by trying |

Interaction is never the only route to the main point.

## Author-led or reader-led

| Follow | When |
| --- | --- |
| A fixed sequence that makes the point | the audience is meeting the subject for the first time, or must reach one conclusion |
| Open exploration | the audience knows the subject and has questions of their own |
| Fixed sequence first, then open exploration | a mixed or unknown audience |

## How many items at once

| Follow | When |
| --- | --- |
| About four | the reader must hold the items in mind together: new concepts before they are used, options to weigh, controls to operate, groups in a navigation |
| As many as needed, in a predictable sorted order | the reader scans to find one item: an index, a reference table, an alphabetical list |

## Design for the scanning eye, or guide it

| Follow | When |
| --- | --- |
| Put the most important words at the top and at the left edge | always; a reader given no cues reads the top lines and then the left edge |
| Add headings, lists and bold key terms that let the reader jump | always, as well; scanning the left edge is what readers fall back on when a page gives them nothing better |

These are one instruction seen from two sides: front-load, and give the reader better landmarks than the left margin.

## Diagram or text

| Follow | When |
| --- | --- |
| A diagram | the reader must see connections, containment, branching or position, and would otherwise search a text for each relation |
| Text: a list or table | the content is a straight sequence, a set of conditions, or exact values; or the diagram would be boxes in a row |

A diagram always comes with a text route to the same structure.

## Worked steps or the bare result

| Follow | When |
| --- | --- |
| A fully worked example, steps and reasons shown | the reader is new to the kind of problem |
| The result and the rule, steps available on request | the reader is experienced; worked steps slow them and can interfere with what they already do |

## Say it twice or say it once

| Follow | When |
| --- | --- |
| Repeat the message in a second form | the forms differ: title and chart, sentence and number, picture and label. Repetition across forms helps recall and reaches more readers |
| Say it once | the repetition is the same words in the same form, or full on-screen text duplicating what a speaker says at the same moment |

Captions and transcripts are the exception that is always provided, under the reader's control.

## Honour a stated preference or follow the content

| Follow | When |
| --- | --- |
| Lead with the form the reader asked for | always, when a reader states a preference |
| Keep the form the content needs | always, as well. Sorting readers into types and giving each type a single form is not supported; offering several forms to everyone is |

## Compact or complete

| Follow | When |
| --- | --- |
| Fit one screen, with the rest one step away | the reader monitors or decides: a dashboard, a one-pager, the top of a doc page |
| One long page with a clear outline | the reader learns or follows a procedure and should not have to assemble it from pieces |

Anything needed for safety or correctness stays in the main flow in both cases.
